%  ABSTRACT
% ============================================================
Extratropical cyclones (ETCs) generate intense winds and substantial precipitation along the Southwestern Atlantic coast, whose spatial coincidence is usually assumed without explicit verification. While the Northern Hemisphere has systematic studies on how these fields are distributed, the Southern Hemisphere lacks an equivalent integration combining spatial density estimation with univariate and multivariate Empirical Orthogonal Function (EOF) analysis by life-cycle phase. This thesis develops such an integration to assess whether ETC dynamic intensity predicts the magnitude and location of its 10-meter wind and precipitation maxima, or whether both fields develop a qualitatively distinct structural organization depending on the evolutionary phase.

The analysis is built on a cyclone-tracking catalog based on 850-hPa relative vorticity \citep{gramcianinov2020analysis} and the objective classification of the life cycle into four phases using \textit{CycloPhaser} \citep{coutodesouza2024new, deSouza2025CycloPhaser}, applied to ERA5 (1979--2020). The sample was stratified by three cyclogenesis regions (SBR, LPB, ARG) and two intensity strata: the Global Population and the subset of the most intense cyclones (p90, drawn from the Global Population). On this basis, probability density functions (PDF) of magnitude, spatial density estimation (PDFe) of location, univariate EOF decomposition with significance assessed via Bootstrap-$t$, and MEOF decomposition of the joint covariance were applied progressively, ultimately integrated into coupled structural prototypes.

In the Global Population, wind behaves passively and predictably, with a single dominant EOF mode (31--57\% of variance) coupled to vorticity; precipitation, in contrast, is distributed across multiple modes (EOF1 only 11--16\%). This architecture reorganizes drastically in the p90 subset: wind concentrates strongly in the northwest quadrant (mode of 23--25~m\,s$^{-1}$, densities up to $27\times10^{-4}$ in SBR), while precipitation collapses at the core and reorganizes into an arc-shaped structure toward the southeast and south. The MEOF shows that both transformations are the same reorganization: in the Global Population it captures 14.8--26.6\% of the joint covariance, with precipitation and wind gradients oriented along perpendicular axes, but in p90 it drops to 11.65--23.47\% and reveals, from maturity onward, a geometric anticovariance pattern, supported by a significant correlation between the principal components of both fields ($|r|$ between $0.31$ and $0.45$). The structural prototypes quantify this separation at over $8^{\circ}$--$9^{\circ}$ during maturity and decay, with ARG reaching the largest magnitude (${\sim}10$--$11^{\circ}$). The magnitude of this decoupling varies regionally without a single identified factor, although its direction, precipitation to the southeast, wind to the northwest, remains consistent across the three regions.

These results show that, in mature ETCs of the Southwestern Atlantic, wind and precipitation maxima operate in opposite quadrants, and that parameterizing them through scalar central-intensity metrics systematically underestimates both the magnitude and the location of both forcings. This work combines spatial PDFe, univariate EOF, and MEOF to quantify this decoupling by evolutionary phase and genesis region in the Southern Hemisphere, with implications for coastal risk management in Brazil, Uruguay, and Argentina.

\noindent \textbf{Keywords:} South America; extratropical cyclones; wind at 10 meters; precipitation; spatial structure; multivariate EOF.
