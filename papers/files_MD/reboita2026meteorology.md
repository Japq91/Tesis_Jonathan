8.1 Introduction
South America (SA) is the fourth-largest continent in the globe and one of the few with a
wide meridional extension (55°S–12°N). This continent is bounded by the South Pacific
Ocean to the west, South Atlantic Ocean to the east, and the North Atlantic Ocean to the
north (Figure 8.1). South America’s physiography is diversified and includes the Andes
Mountains, the world’s longest mountain range stretching from Colombia (~10°N) to the
southernmost tip of SA (~53°S), along its western border, the Amazon tropical forest, the
world’s largest tropical rainforest occupying 35% of the continental area, and the Amazon
River, the world’s largest river in terms of discharge and drainage area (Gupta, 2022).
Considering the length, the Amazon River (6,000 km) is only shorter than the Nile River
(6,500 km) in Africa (Gupta, 2022). Additionally, the Amazon’s humid climate is respon-
sible for supplying about 20% of the world’s fresh water and plays a crucial role in global
energy, moisture, and carbon balances (Phillips et al., 2017; Baudena et al., 2021), as well
as hosting the most prominent global biodiversity and immense ethnic and cultural rich-
ness (Gorenflo et al., 2012). SA is also home to the sixth-largest hydrological basin in the
world, the La Plata basin (Gupta, 2022). Expressive contrasts in water availability mark the
continent, with one of the world’s wettest regions off the Pacific coast in western Colombia
(Mejía et al., 2021) and the driest place in the world, the Atacama Desert (Böhm et al.,
2020; González-Pinilla et al., 2021). Another peculiar region in SA is a sub-equatorial dry-
land in northeastern Brazil embedded in a continental tropical zone, known as the Brazilian
semi-arid (Hastenrath, 2012; Reboita et al., 2016; Marengo et al., 2020).
Covering extensive latitudinal zones, a combination of extratropical, subtropical, and
tropical weather systems acts over SA, leading to different climate regimes (Ferreira and
Reboita, 2022). These regimes are illustrated in Figure 8.1, with climographs (annual cycle
of precipitation and near-surface temperature) calculated for the areas indicated by black
boxes. Over southern Amazon, midwestern and southeastern Brazil, the climate follows
a monsoon regime (Zhou and Lau, 1998; Gan et al., 2004; Vera et al., 2006; Reboita
et al., 2010a; Liebmann and Mechoso, 2011; Marengo et al., 2012; Grimm, 2019a; Grimm
et al., 2020; Sena and Magnusdottir, 2020; Teodoro et al., 2021; Ferreira and Reboita,
2022), characterised by a well-defined rainy season from November to March, and a dry

8.2 South America Climatology: Atmospheric Circulation,
Moisture Transport, Precipitation, and Temperature
According to the first definitions of a monsoon region (Ramage, 1971), SA is not con-
sidered one, given that the circulation does not exhibit seasonal low-level wind reversal
between the wet (represented by January) and dry seasons (represented by July). This pat-
tern is also evident in modern reanalysis seasonal means (Figure 8.2a–d).
The low-level wind reversal over SA appears only when the annual mean is removed
from the seasonal mean (Zhou and Lau, 1998), as shown in Figure 8.2e–h. Modern defini-
tions to characterise a monsoon region are based on the difference in precipitation between
summer and winter extended seasons, where most of the annual precipitation (>55%)
occurs in extended summer (Wang et al., 2012). Applying this definition, most tropical
and subtropical (Bolivia, Paraguay, and North Argentina) SA presents monsoon features
(Figure 8.3a). Silva and Kousky (2012) also highlighted that portions of central and eastern
Brazil and the Andes Mountains (12°S–32°S) receive more than 50% of the total annual
precipitation during DJF and less than 5% during JJA. The combination of atmospheric
systems responsible for these wet features over the continent receives the name of the
South American Monsoon System (SAMS), as described later.
From the austral winter to summer, the continental surface heating increases. Over the
subtropics, it helps to develop the Chaco thermal low (located between North Argentina,
Paraguay, and southern Bolivia) and the Northwest Argentina thermal-orographic low
(Seluchi and Saulo, 2012; Arraut et al., 2023; Figure 8.2e–h). At the same time, the trade
winds intensify, increasing the moisture transport from the tropical North Atlantic (TNA)
into the Amazon basin (Durán-Quesada et al., 2012; Drumond et al., 2014; Leyba et al.,
2023) and, consequently, intensifying the moisture transport by the low-level jet (LLJ)
east of the Andes, also known as the South American low-level jet (SALLJ). The combi-
nation of the local convection with the moisture transport from the tropics by the SALLJ
and from the tropical Atlantic Ocean by the winds over the western flank of the South
Atlantic Subtropical Anticyclone (SASA; Drumond et al., 2008; Reboita et al., 2019a;
Leyba et al., 2023) contributes to developing a persistent cloudy and rainy band called the
South Atlantic Convergence Zone (SACZ; Carvalho et al., 2004; more details in Section
8.3.1). Low-pressure systems near the east coast of Brazil, such as cold fronts or subtropi-
cal/extratropical cyclones, help to channel the humidity from the continent into the ocean,
strengthening the continental convection and the SACZ organisation (Silva et al., 2019;

Another characteristic of the SAMS is the upper-level high-pressure system over
Bolivia, which is a response to the intense convection in southern Amazonia that forces
an intense divergence in the upper troposphere and, consequently, a semi-stationary
planetary Rossby wave (Silva Dias et al., 1983; Karoly, 1989). This wave pattern
explains the location of the Bolivian High (BH) not over the maximum of convec-
tion (precipitation) in southern Amazonia but displaced southwestward over Bolivia
(Lenters and Cook, 1997; Figure 8.3a). By conservation of absolute vorticity, an upper-
level trough develops downstream from the BH near northeastern Brazil, from which
it receives its name, the Northeast Brazil trough (Kousky and Gan, 1981); however, it
can assume different positions, such as over the tropical South Atlantic (TSA) (Ferreira
et al., 2019). On some occasions, this trough intensifies and allows the development of
an upper-level cyclonic vortex (Ferreira et al., 2019; Morais et al., 2020). This closed
vortex presents a cold core and, in general, an absence of clouds, while its periphery is
characterised by intense convective activity (Kousky and Gan, 1981). Much of the SA
precipitation during the wet period is also a response to the diffluent flow between the
BH and the downstream trough.

without SACZ events). When a climatology that only has SACZ events is considered

8.3a. The mid-upper level circulation, besides the BH and the Northeast Brazil trough,
shows a trough around southern Brazil, Uruguay, and the Atlantic Ocean and a ridge

The ridge-trough circulation over the subtropics is the typical SACZ pattern and is
not observed when the SACZ is inactive. However, the daily atmospheric circulation
during SACZ episodes can sometimes be different from the typical ones (Seluchi and
Chou, 2009; Escobar and Reboita, 2022). For instance, Escobar and Reboita (2022)
described four atmospheric circulation patterns associated with SACZ episodes. In
three of them, the SACZ is modulated by the propagation of a cold front towards
southeastern Brazil and the varying positions of the low- and high-pressure systems
associated with the front, which, consequently, modifies the mid-upper level circula-
tion. The fourth pattern is associated with the presence of a cut-off low centred over
the central-southern region of Brazil.

Over the Brazilian semi-arid, the second driest region in SA, the aridity results from
the interaction between local morphology and large-scale dynamical mechanisms.
In this region, the total annual precipitation is up to 75% lower than along the coast
of Northeast Brazil (Reboita et al., 2016). Vertical profiles of zonal and meridional
circulation centred on the Borborema Plateau (a discontinuous region of high eleva-
tions located in the centre of Northeast Brazil) show that the aridity over the region
(Figure 8.1k) is caused mainly by adiabatic compression. This subsidence is intensified
by the descending branches of the Walker (subsidence branch of the zonal circulation
cell formed by intense convective activity in the Amazon basin) and Hadley (ITCZ’s
descending branch when displaced to its most boreal position) circulations, both located
over the region, especially in winter (Reboita et al., 2016). However, not all of Northeast
Brazil is a semi-arid region. During the whole year, the Subtropical Atlantic Ocean is a
moisture source to Northeast Brazil and eastern Amazonia (Figure 8.4; Drumond et al.,
2010, 2019). Even with moisture, it is necessary a trigger to organise precipitation.
Between February and May, the Atlantic ITCZ moves southward of its climatological
position, reaching around 4°S (Uvo et al., 1998; Utida et al., 2019; Nogueira et al.,
2024a) promoting precipitation in the north and northeast parts of Northeast Brazil.
Additionally, the southerly displacement of the ITCZ triggers the development of trop-
ical mesoscale convective complexes (MCCs), which are responsible for intense pre-
cipitation in the continental area (Lyra et al., 2020). As the ITCZ migrates northward,
its descending branch is a potential precipitation inhibitor in the region. Coastal areas
of Northeast Brazil have precipitation associated with sea-land breezes and trade winds
convergence (Kousky, 1980). During the day, a maximum of rainfall occurs 100–300
km inland associated with the sea breeze that develops and advances in the continent’s
interior and merges with convection occurring over higher topography (Kousky, 1980).
Analysis using station data established that in the state of Alagoas (Northeast Brazil),
the rainfall begins near the coast during the morning (11–12 UTC) and advances inland,
covering a distance of ~250 km from the coast, two hours later (Silva, 2022). As shown
in da Rocha et al. (2009) and Custódio (2013), the daytime rainfall (from 13 to 23 UTC)
in Northeast Brazil represents ~55% of the seasonal rainfall, and it may be associated
with the sea breeze during the year (Silva, 2022). In austral winter (JJA), the rainfall
identified in the climatology along the eastern coast of the Northeast (Figure 8.2c) also
contributes substantially to the convergence between the offshore land breeze and the
opposite flow of the trade winds (Kousky, 1980; Teixeira, 2008; Anjos and Lopes,
2019).

coast during the rainy season (between April and July) and transport large amounts of

Gozzo et al., 2014; Crespo et al., 2021; de Jesus et al., 2021). A larger number of cyclones
and their associated cold fronts cause rainfall in the mentioned areas. From mid-spring to
mid-autumn, precipitation also has the contribution of convection and MCSs. During these

advected by the SASA northward flow.
8.3 Atmospheric Systems
8.3.1 Convergence Zones: ITCZ and SACZ
Globally, large-scale precipitation events occur over areas with the convergence of mois-
ture flux, easily identified in satellite images as areas of extensive cloudiness. The most
prominent convergence zone is the ITCZ, a zonally elongated cloud band over the tropi-
cal oceans (Krishnamurti et al., 2013; see also Chapter 5). Convergence zones also form
along the subtropical jet, at the eastern flank of an upper-level trough, with the moisture
advected into the region by a poleward flow on the western sector of the subtropical highs
(Kodama, 1993). There are three such regions in the Southern Hemisphere: the South Indian
Convergence Zone (SICZ) over southern Africa (discussed in Chapter 9), the South Pacific
Convergence Zone (SPCZ) over the western and central South Pacific Ocean (discussed
in Chapter 10), and the SACZ over SA (discussed here). It is very likely that the convec-
tive activity over one of the subtropical convergence zones affects the other. However, no
comprehensive study analysed their interactions. Nonetheless, the convective activity over
the SPCZ triggers a Rossby wave propagating across the South Pacific, which may affect
the SACZ (Hirata and Grimm, 2016). In turn, convection over the SACZ modulates the
regional circulation over southern Africa, favouring the development of the SICZ (Grimm
and Reason, 2015).
Over SA, the ITCZ seasonal cycle modulates the precipitation along the continent’s
west and east tropical regions. In the tropical Pacific, off the western coast of SA, the ITCZ
is located mainly north of 5°N, except during El Niño events, when it is shifted west and
south (Poveda et al., 2006). In the tropical Atlantic, off the eastern coast of SA, during aus-
tral winter and spring, the ITCZ is at its northernmost position around 10°N, contributing
to the precipitation over the Upper Amazon and Orinoco basins, the Guianas, Colombia,
and Central America (Hastenrath, 1991; Poveda et al., 2006). At the end of austral spring,
the ITCZ migrates to the south, contributing to the onset of the SAMS from September
to November (Zhou and Lau, 2001). In late summer and early fall (February to April),
the ITCZ reaches its southernmost position (~4°S) in the west of the TSA, which defines
the main rainy season over most of Northeast Brazil (Hastenrath, 1991; Uvo et al., 1998;

The SACZ activity is responsible for a large fraction of the monsoonal precipitation (Zilli
and Hart, 2021) that results from the combination of tropical convection with extratropical
instabilities associated with the propagation of midlatitude Rossby waves forced by sea
surface temperature (SST) anomalies. Over the tropics, numerical experiments suggest that
the convection over the Amazon provides the moisture necessary for the organisation of the
SACZ (Figueroa et al., 1995; Gandu and Silva Dias, 1998). Over the extratropics, a wave
train bridges over the South Pacific, connecting the SPCZ activity to the occurrence of a
SACZ 10 days later (Grimm and Silva Dias, 1995; Hirata and Grimm, 2016). As the midlat-
itude Rossby waves reach the southern tip of SA, the waves are deflected equatorward along
the east coast of SA, forming an upper-level trough on the subtropical jet, with the SACZ on
its leading edge (Carvalho et al., 2011; Gonzalez and Vera, 2014). As it propagates equator-
ward, the Rossby wave interacts with the strong wind shear along the equatorward flank of
the subtropical jet, resulting in its elongation and consequent diagonal feature (van der Wiel
et al., 2015). When approaching the southeastern coast of Brazil, the vorticity anomalies
advected by the disturbances generate areas of energy accumulation that stall the propaga-
tion of the Rossby wave, resulting in the SACZ persisting longer over the region (van der
Wiel et al., 2015; Hirata and Grimm, 2016; Zilli and Hart, 2021). The SACZ extension over
the adjacent subtropical South Atlantic Ocean cools the sea surface and weakens the Brazil
Current, modifying the ocean dynamics (Pezzi et al., 2022). The cooling results from a com-
bination of reduced solar shortwave radiation reaching the sea surface due to the cloudiness
(Almeida et al., 2007) with a strengthened cyclonic mesoscale eddy in the ocean driven by
the atmospheric circulation that decreases the southward heat transport by the Brazil Current
(Pezzi et al., 2022). The cooling effect extends southwestward of the SACZ due to the pres-
ence of an atmospheric cyclonic vortex that favours divergent Ekman transport and offshore
upwelling. Together, these mechanisms can increase the persistence of the SACZ over the
region (Rosa et al., 2020; Fialho et al., 2023).
The anomalous heat source over the SACZ increases the vorticity tendency over the
region, resulting in a source of Rossby wave activity that can modulate the circulation
anomalies downstream (Grimm and Reason, 2015; Zilli and Hart, 2021). Over the Southern
Hemisphere, the wave packets originating over the South Atlantic Ocean off the eastern
Brazilian coast can trigger ridging events that contribute to intense precipitation over South
Africa (Ndarana et al., 2022). Numerical experiments also suggest that during neutral and
El Niño years, the heat source related to the SACZ can generate a perturbation that prop-
agates northeastward across the North Atlantic Ocean towards the subtropics in northern
Africa and Asia (Braga et al., 2024).

8.3.2 Atmospheric Rivers and Low-Level Jets
Atmospheric rivers (ARs) and LLJs are two dynamical/meteorological structures
known to play important roles in atmospheric moisture transport, bringing large
amounts of precipitation and often contributing to extreme precipitation (Gimeno
et al., 2016). ARs are relatively shallow (1–2.5 km in height) and narrow (300–500
km in width) atmospheric moisture corridors stretching over distances of at least 2,000
km. They are often associated with the warm conveyor belt (WCB) of extratropical
cyclones (Carlson, 1991) and generally affect the western continents. Along the SA
Pacific coast, AR landfalls are most frequent between 38°S and 50°S, occurring 35–40
days yr−1 (Viale et al., 2018). In Chile, ARs contribute to 45–60% of the annual pre-
cipitation in subtropics (37°–32°S) and 40–55% along the midlatitude west coast (37°–
47°S) (Viale et al., 2018). Over subtropical western SA, few studies have documented
landfalling ARs and associated orographic precipitation processes (Garreaud, 2013;
Viale et al., 2013).
Low-level jets are a core of maximum winds with intensity higher than a specific
threshold at lower levels of the atmosphere, and they do not have restrictions of width,
length, and distance as the ARs. In SA, SALLJ (near the central Andes in Bolivia and
Paraguay) is the most studied LLJ, but in recent years, attention has also been given to
the Orinoco low-level jet (OLLJ) occurring over Colombia and Venezuela (Jiménez-
Sánchez et al., 2020; Martinez et al., 2022), which is sometimes referred to as the
northern branch of the SALLJ (Jones, 2019; Espinoza et al., 2020; Builes-Jamarillo
et al., 2022), the coastal low-level jet (CLLJ) over the western slope of the Chilean
Andes (Garreaud and Muñoz, 2005; Aguirre et al., 2021), and the Chocó LLJ, one of

the OLLJ is weaker, resulting in larger precipitation rates over the region (Espinoza

of rainfall.

dominated by the SPSA, with its low-level southerly flow forming the CLLJ. This jet is

position (30°S to 37°S). The surface stress exerted by this flow promotes the upwelling of
cold water, resulting in the equatorward advection of nutrient-rich waters and supporting
the fishery industry over the region (Garreaud and Muñoz, 2005).
Closer to the equator, the interaction between the trade winds and Colombia’s north-
south coast forms a unique southwesterly flow denominated the Chocó Jet (Poveda and
Mesa, 2000). The southeasterly trade winds cross the equator over the eastern Pacific and
veer clockwise due to changes in the sign of the Coriolis acceleration, forming the Chocó
Jet. The combination of the convergence between the westerly flow and the higher-level
easterly trade winds (~700 hPa), low surface pressure, moist environment, and the local
topography results in the development of deep convection characteristic of the region
(Poveda and Mesa, 2000). The Chocó Jet is prevalent between September and November
(Poveda et al., 2014), when it is associated with forming MCSs over the region (Sakamoto
et al., 2011).

20°S and 60°S, with a maximum at ~38°S (Figure 8.7a), and are more frequent near
western SA during autumn and winter (Fuenzalida et al., 2005; Campetella and Possia,
2007; Reboita et al., 2010c; Pinheiro et al., 2017). COLs originating upstream of the
Andes Mountains have a large contribution from ageostrophic fluxes, while the ones
in the eastern Andes are mainly driven by baroclinic processes (Pinheiro et al., 2022).
In the coastal western subtropical Andes, COLs contribute up to 50% of the annual
rainfall (e.g., Aceituno et al., 2021; Muñoz and Schultz, 2021). COLs of tropical ori-
gin, generally called tropical CVs, are associated with upper-level troughs that develop
downstream of the BH in the surroundings of Northeast Brazil (Figure 8.7a) during the
wet phase of the SA monsoon (Kousky and Gan, 1981; Mishra et al., 2001; Reboita
et al., 2010c; Ferreira et al., 2019). While the absence of precipitation characterises the
regions under the COLs’ centre, higher accumulated precipitation generally occurs in
its periphery (e.g., Lyra and Arraut, 2020).
8.3.4 Mesoscale Convective Systems
South America also experiences a large number of MCSs per year. MCSs are systems
with horizontal dimensions from a few to several hundred kilometres and durations
from hours to one day (Schumacher and Rasmussen, 2020). MCSs can be a single
convective cell or a cluster of clouds organised in lines (squall lines), near-circular
format (MCCs), or without a defined form. In SA, MCSs can have a subtropical or
tropical origin.
The subtropical region composed of northern Argentina, Paraguay, Uruguay, and
southern Brazil (La Plata Basin) presents the highest number of hours per year with ade-
quate environmental conditions for the development of severe thunderstorms in the world
(Taszarek et al., 2021). In addition, this same region is also a global hot spot for severe
storms (Cecil and Blankenship, 2012; dos Santos et al., 2023) and tornadoes (Tippett et al.,
2015). All these features are, in general, a consequence of MCSs (Salio et al., 2007; Feng
et al., 2021).
Figure 8.8 illustrates the development of a typical MCS over the subtropics of SA
on 16 October 2023, highlighting the rapid development of the system and its eastward
displacement. The genesis of these systems is associated with the combination of the
moisture and warm air transported from the tropics to the subtropics by the SALLJ and
by the divergence aloft associated with the jetstream (Silva Dias, 1987; Martinez and
Solman, 2022). Hence, MCSs genesis occurs in a region with intense vertical shear of
the horizontal wind, which, between 0 and 6 km, can reach 16.28 m s−1 (Rasera et al.,
2018). Over Argentina, MCSs show a maximum of latent heating at 6 km height (Liu
et al., 2021).

8.3.5 Rossby Wave Breaking, Blockings, and Extremes
In the South American-Atlantic sector, persistent large-scale Rossby wave breaking
tends to occur in the subtropical region during the austral summer when the jet is
positioned farthest poleward. The wave-breaking structure is not equivalent barotropic
and does not block the westerly flow over subtropical eastern SA (Berrisford et al.,
2007; Rodrigues and Woollings, 2017; Mendes et al., 2023). As such, these subtropical
wave-breaking episodes are not considered examples of traditional midlatitude block-
ing and are not detected by the commonly used methodology based on the reversal of
the meridional geopotential height gradient (Mendes and Cavalcanti, 2014; Oliveira
et al., 2014). Dynamically, they are considered subtropical ridges (Sousa et al., 2021)
and are better detected by a methodology that uses potential vorticity (Berrisford et al.,
2007).
Nevertheless, Rossby wave breaking over subtropical SA is associated with a persistent
anticyclonic circulation that leads to deficient rainfall and temperature extremes with dev-
astating socioeconomic consequences. For simplicity, here we will refer to large-scale
wave-breaking episodes as atmospheric blocking. Since it is more prominent during the
months of austral summer, atmospheric blocking in this region prevents the development of
the SACZ, affecting the wet season of the SAMS. The frequency of blocking days is highly
anti-correlated to the rainfall in eastern SA. The persistence of these events (i.e., with many
consecutive days of blocking) is also important to generate the extremes.
The lack of clouds in the summer due to blocking can lead to temperature increases
both on land and in the ocean. Up to 80% of warm surface air temperature extremes
occur simultaneously with blocking events (Rodrigues and Woollings, 2017). The asso-
ciated decrease in cloud cover increases shortwave radiation at the land and ocean’s
surface. At the same time, weaker winds decrease evaporative cooling. Combined, these

8.4 Teleconnection Patterns and Precipitation
The climatology of SA shown in Figure 8.1 can be greatly modified by different telecon-
nection patterns (see Chapter 11 for details on climate modes and Reboita et al., 2021c, for
a review on their impacts on SA precipitation).
On intraseasonal timescales, the strongest influence on the SA monsoon comes from
the Madden–Julian Oscillation (MJO). This mode of climate variability has a frequency
of 30–60 days (Madden and Julian, 1971), alternating patterns of enhanced and sup-
pressed tropical rainfall as it moves eastward along the tropics, resulting in eight dis-
tinct phases. The MJO modulates tropical cyclone genesis, intensity, and landfall in
the Atlantic Ocean (Haertel, 2023). Many of these systems cause rainfall and other
atmospheric disturbances in the extreme north of SA. Over tropical SA, precipitation
is favoured in the MJO phases 7, 8, 1, and 2, and suppressed in phases 3, 4, 5, and 6
(additional details can be found in Chapters 5 and 11). According to Grimm (2019b),
MJO in phase 1 contributes to increase the average daily precipitation by more than
30% of the climatological value, and it can be a response of extratropical teleconnection
via Rossby waves beginning in the central subtropical South Pacific Ocean. It leads to a
cyclonic anomaly over subtropical SA, favouring moisture convergence and uplift that,
consequently, enhances the SACZ (Grimm, 2019b). MJO also affects the persistence of
intense SACZ episodes and contributes to an increase in the extreme daily rainfall over
eastern tropical Brazil, when the convection is suppressed over Indonesia and enhanced
over the central Pacific (Carvalho et al., 2004).
Both MJO and ENSO contribute to the seesaw of dry and wet conditions over tropical
and subtropical SA (Nogués-Paegle and Mo, 1997). ENSO is the main phenomenon con-
tributing to year-to-year precipitation variability in SA. The positive ENSO phase, or El
Niño, is generally associated with a decrease in precipitation over the extreme north of SA,
north of Northeast Brazil, and east of Amazonia as a response to the subsidence intensi-
fication in these regions due to changes in the Walker circulation. On the other hand, an
increase in precipitation is expected on the coast of Ecuador and Peru during El Niño. In
the extratropics, El Niño events contribute to increasing both the occurrence and intensity

intensification of the southeastern trade winds, which configures a coupling mechanism.

Considering the climate variability modes with lower frequency scales, the Pacific
Decadal Oscillation (PDO; Mantua et al., 1997; Newman et al., 2016) is a pattern that,

pattern on SA climate (Wang et al., 2014). Andreoli and Kayano (2005) showed that El
Niño and the warm phase of PDO (characterised by positive SST anomalies in the tropi-
cal Pacific Ocean) result in strong negative precipitation anomalies over northeast Brazil
and excessive precipitation in the southern region, whereas El Niño and the PDO cold
phase lead to weaker precipitation anomalies in SA. Analysis of the stream function at 200
hPa, conducted by Andreoli and Kayano (2005), revealed that the precipitation anomalies
during the PDO warm phase can be explained by a cyclonic centre over eastern and north-
eastern Brazil and a weak anticyclonic centre over southeastern SA.
The PDO has been recognised as the North Pacific node of the Interdecadal Pacific
Oscillation (IPO; Power et al., 1999; Folland et al., 2002), which exhibits a tripolar struc-
ture. It consists of one centre of SST anomalies in the tropics and another centre with an
opposite signal in the extratropics of each hemisphere (refer to Figure 1 from Henley et al.,
2015). The phases of the IPO appear to be associated with trends in global mean surface
temperatures (Meehl et al., 2016). The positive phase of the IPO, characterised by warmer
waters in the eastern equatorial Pacific and along the west coast of North America, as well
as colder waters in the extratropics, coincides with periods of rapid warming from 1910 to
1941 and 1971 to 1995. Porter et al. (2021) mention that many authors suggest the PDO
and the IPO are comparable in describing Pacific climate variability. Therefore, the term
Pacific decadal variability is used to encompass both phenomena. In fact, the IPO precip-
itation anomalies presented by Buckley et al. (2019) in the global tropical band show for
SA a similar pattern in the anomalies to those when the PDO and El Niño are in phase.
Furthermore, Lopez et al. (2016) demonstrate that the positive phase of the IPO modulates
the SASA through stationary Rossby waves, which also will influence the rainfall in the
eastern part of Brazil. According to these authors, the primary regions of Rossby wave

8.5 Climate Change: From Past to Future
This section covers topics on palaeoclimate, current climate, and future projections.
Discussing palaeoclimate is a challenging task since the history of the planet is estimated
to be 4.5–4.6 billion years, implicating different geological eras (Precambrian, Palaeozoic,
Mesozoic, and Cenozoic), which are divided into several periods with their peculiarities
(NPS, 2024). For this reason, only the South American monsoon in the Quaternary is
covered here. Mohtadi et al. (2016) highlight that the palaeoclimatic evidence of monsoon
rainfall could help in understanding and predicting the monsoon sensitivity to various forc-
ing mechanisms.
According to Prado et al. (2013), the mid-Holocene (around 6,000 years ago) is a key
period in palaeoclimate because it experienced lower incoming summer insolation com-
pared to the late Holocene in the Southern Hemisphere, thus affecting the SA climate. For
the mid-Holocene (around 6,000 years ago), Gorenstein et al. (2022) found reduced rain-
fall in Amazonia and a warmer and drier southern SA, indicating an overall weakening of
the wet monsoon period, which is most likely caused by changes in precipitation over the
SACZ and a weaker than present SASA. Summer monsoon was also characterised by tem-
poral variabilities during the last 1,600 years. For instance, during the Medieval Climate
Anomaly period, monsoon was dominated by dry anomalies (Apaéstegui et al., 2014),
while during the Little Ice Age, wet conditions predominated over the central-western
tropical SA and a displacement of the SACZ to the southwest (Orrison et al., 2022). These
changes agree with Della Libera et al. (2022), who found a retraction and expansion of the
Amazon forest during the Medieval Climate Anomaly and Little Ice Age, respectively.
Morales et al. (2020) investigated the climate of the region south of 12°S and west of
50°W in the last 600 years, and they found an increase in severe hydroclimatic events
since the mid-twentieth century. According to the literature review by Baker and Fritz
(2015), ENSO is insufficient to explain the SA monsoon variability observed in both the
instrumental and the palaeoclimate records, highlighting the important role of the tropical
Atlantic SST in explaining much of the variability.
Moving to recently observed trends in near-surface variables, studies have been indi-
cating a positive and statistically significant trend in air temperature in most of SA – the
exception is the northeast of Argentina – while precipitation signal trends depend on the
region (Soares et al., 2017; Dunn et al., 2020; Reboita et al., 2022c; Santos et al., 2023).

Many studies have evaluated the performance of global climate models in reproducing
observed climatic features in SA (e.g., Almazroui et al., 2021; Dias and Reboita, 2021;
Ortega et al., 2021; Collazo et al., 2022; Firpo et al., 2022; Bazzanela et al., 2023; Reboita
et al., 2024). They have shown that models participating in the Coupled Intercomparison
Project Phase 5 (CMIP5) and/or 6 (CMIP6) capture the main observed climate character-
istics across the continent, particularly the large-scale features of the SAMS (Jones and
Carvalho, 2013; Pascale et al., 2019), such as the dipolar structure characteristic of intrase-
asonal and interannual variability associated with the SACZ (Diaz and Vera, 2017), but the
models have low skill to simulate the SACZ position and related precipitation (Carvalho
and Jones, 2013; Zilli and Carvalho, 2021).
The chaotic nature of the climate system as well as the observational restrictions limit
the performance of global models over the continent. For example, CMIP6 historical simu-
lations delay the onset of the SAMS due to deficiencies in the models’ representation of the
circulation patterns that modulate the precipitation variability during the transition season,
including those related to the SACZ (Olmo et al., 2022). CMIP5 models have difficulties
reproducing the north-south movement of the Atlantic ITCZ, placing the maximum ITCZ-
related precipitation either over the western equatorial Atlantic or over the eastern basin
(Siongco et al., 2015). Despite some improvements in the intensity of the precipitation,
similar biases also occur in the CMIP6 models. Over the Pacific, the main deficiency is the
presence of the double-ITCZ in both CMIP5 and CMIP6 simulations (Ortega et al., 2021).
In the extratropics, biases in the upper-level westerly winds over the central South Pacific
modify the structure of the Rossby waves propagating into SA, resulting in a reduction in
the number of tropical-extratropical cloud bands, including SACZ events, in October and
November (Zilli et al., 2019, 2023).
The deficiencies in global climate models, as well as their inability to simulate regional
climate features due to the coarse resolution, have boosted the use of different regional
climate models (RCMs) in the SA domain, such as Eta, REMO, Weather Research and
Forecasting (WRF), Regional Climate Model (RegCM), and the Rossby Centre regional
climate model (RCA). da Rocha et al. (2009), Solman et al. (2013), Chou et al. (2014),
Ambrizzi et al. (2018), Llopart et al. (2020), Teodoro et al. (2021), Lagos-Zúñiga et al.
(2022), and Reboita et al. (2022b, 2022c) have highlighted that these RCMs can represent
the SA climate adding value to the global climate models, although some persistent bias
still remains, as a warm bias in the subtropical region. RCM simulations also may not
improve the GCMs’ performance regarding the SACZ-related dipolar structure in syn-
optic, intraseasonal, and interannual scales (Solman and Blazquez, 2019). In most cases,
the skill of the RCM simulations in reproducing the SACZ-related precipitation is highly
dependent on the GCM skill. Despite these RCM weaknesses, they are the best tools to get
information related to the future climate.

For the end of the century, global and regional climate models consistently project
an increase in the air temperature over SA. In more pessimistic scenarios (RCP8.5 and
SSP5-8.5), the warming can exceed 6°C in western Amazonia (Lagos-Zúñiga et al.,
2022; Reboita et al., 2022c) and 1.5°C in Patagonia (Pessacg et al., 2020). The pro-
jected changes in precipitation are less consistent among the models, even though many
of them project dry and wet conditions, respectively, over the Amazon and La Plata
basins. A multi-RCM projection study by Reboita et al. (2022b) suggests that under the
RCP8.5 scenario, the Amazon will be more susceptible to dry conditions, which will be
accompanied by a decrease in precipitation intensity and in the number of CWD; in parts
of southeastern SA, both total precipitation and the frequency of extreme daily rainfall
events will increase.
Southeastern and midwestern Brazil are the regions with more uncertainties in the
future precipitation since some models, such as Eta, project dry conditions, while RegCM,
REMO, and others project wet conditions (Llopart et al., 2020; Reboita et al., 2022b). In
these regions, global and regional models also diverge in relation to water resources’ trends
signal (Llopart et al., 2020). Projections for Patagonia indicate a precipitation decrease
(from −10 to −30%), and it is a robust signal since there is agreement among the models
and consistent physical drivers associated with the changes (Pessacg et al., 2020). For the
SAMS (Figure 8.3a), RegCM projects no expansion or reduction in the area with mon-
soon features for the end of the century (Teodoro et al., 2021). However, its lifetime will
decrease associated with a delay in the beginning and an anticipation of the wet period
retreat (Asfahq et al., 2021; Reboita et al., 2022d).
There are few studies on future projections of cyclones over the surrounding oceans of
SA, and most of them used RegCM under the RCP8.5 scenario, focusing on the southwest-
ern South Atlantic Ocean. Extratropical cyclones are projected to decrease in frequency,
while more intense ones and those reaching the explosive status (pressure deepening of
~24 hPa/24 h) are projected to become more frequent (Reboita et al., 2021b, 2021e; de
Jesus et al., 2021). For subtropical cyclones, frequency is projected to decrease concomi-
tantly with a strengthening of the most intense ones (de Jesus et al., 2022). Additionally,
using RegCM, Glazer et al. (2021) found that the large-scale environment proper to thun-
derstorms in the subtropics of SA will lead to a higher frequency and intensity of thunder-
storms, which agrees with the findings of Taszarek et al. (2021) and Lapore et al. (2021).
8.6 Gaps and Challenges
Despite large advancements in understanding the SA climate over the last decades, some
areas still need further development. The Global Observing System (GOS) of surface
and upper-level measurements (by weather stations and radiosondes, respectively) is still
heterogeneous, with large gaps over remote areas such as the Amazon and the Andes.
Comparisons among different observed gridded precipitation datasets (interpolated rain
gauge stations and satellite estimates) and reanalysis (climate model assimilating observa-
tions) still present large differences, making it difficult to understand the climate variability
and the validation of numerical simulations (Cavazos et al., 2024).

The drivers of double ITCZ need additional attention. There are no theories to com-

is a global problem. ARs are generally defined based on phenomenological descriptions
and objective threshold-based methods and have not changed since their initial description
(Zhu and Newell, 1998); however, this does not guarantee that all so-called ARs follow the
same meteorological patterns (see Gimeno et al., 2014, for a review).
The consequences of the land-use change over the Amazon and midwest Brazil for the
moisture transport to other regions, including southeastern SA, deserve more investigation.
Also, how changes in the convection over the Amazon due to deforestation can affect the
Hadley and Walker circulations.
An exhaustive analysis of the physical processes associated with explosive extratropical
and subtropical cyclone development is still needed, mainly in the South Pacific Ocean. In
addition, the mesoscale structures embedded in these systems need more attention.
Most studies discussed in this chapter focus on SA or specific regions within the conti-
nent. However, few efforts have been made to understand how the convection associated
with the SAMS can modulate synoptic and intraseasonal disturbances over southern Africa
and the Indian Ocean, such as those explored by Ramos et al. (2019). On larger timescales,
understanding the combined influence of different teleconnection patterns on the SA cli-
mate is essential to improve subseasonal and seasonal forecasts. For example, Freitas et al.
(2023) showed that a large hydrological drought in the Paraná River Basin had different
combinations of drivers over the years.
It is still necessary to improve the knowledge of the phenomena that control climate
variability in midwestern and southeastern Brazil since these areas have low predictabil-
ity in climate forecasts and large uncertain/diverging climate trend signals between future
projections. Due to these uncertainties, the SAMS lifecycle projections still remain a chal-
lenge. These recommendations also indicate the necessity of more studies to improve the
subseasonal to seasonal scales of predictability in SA.
